# Voice and agency

Voice choices determine which participant the sentence treats as its subject. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶76; [[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶82)

## Key points

- Strunk recommends active voice for its usual directness and vigour; removing the agent from a passive can instead make the actor indefinite. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶76; [[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶82)
- Strunk explicitly retains convenient and necessary passives. His choice can depend on which subject the paragraph is discussing. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶83; [[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶86)
- Quiller-Couch attacks a padded recipient construction with an active-verb example; this supports economical agency, rather than establishing a ban on every passive. ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 133)

## In their words

> "The active voice is usually more direct and vigorous than the passive:" — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶76)

> "it becomes indefinite: is it the writer, or some person undisclosed, or the world at large, that will always remember this visit?" — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶82)

> "This rule does not, of course, mean that the writer should entirely discard the passive voice, which is frequently convenient and sometimes necessary." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶83)

> "The first would be the right form in a paragraph on the dramatists of the Restoration; the second, in a paragraph on the tastes of modern readers." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶86)
