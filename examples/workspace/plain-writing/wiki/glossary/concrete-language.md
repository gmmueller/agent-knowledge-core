# Concrete language

Concrete language names the particulars behind an abstraction. ([[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶6; [[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 121)

## Key points

- The Fowlers’ concrete-word preference and Quiller-Couch’s attack on vague abstract nouns converge on identifiable particulars. ([[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶6; [[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 121)
- Quiller-Couch’s image discussion shows that an abstraction can remain while being made visible through a concrete comparison. ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 138)
- Strunk’s equivalent aim is definite statement rather than vague generality. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-misuse-wikisource-the-free-online-libr]] @ ¶2)

## In their words

> "The first is that it uses circumlocution rather than short straight speech." — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 121)

> "Prefer the familiar word to the far-fetched.Prefer the concrete word to the abstract.Prefer the single word to the circumlocution.Prefer the short word to the long.Prefer the Saxon word to the Romance.[1]" — H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler ([[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶6)

> "he solidifies it in tangible stone." — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 138)
