# Circumlocution

A roundabout expression can conceal a thought that direct speech would reveal. ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 121; [[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶6; [[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-misuse-wikisource-the-free-online-libr]] @ ¶10)

## Key points

- Quiller-Couch calls circumlocution an alternative to short straight speech. The Fowlers prefer a single word when it serves, and Strunk reduces as to whether to whether. ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 121; [[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶6; [[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-misuse-wikisource-the-free-online-libr]] @ ¶10)
- Quiller-Couch flags as regards and similar phrases as evasions of a simple statement. ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 130)

## In their words

> "The first is that it uses circumlocution rather than short straight speech." — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 121)

> "Prefer the familiar word to the far-fetched.Prefer the concrete word to the abstract.Prefer the single word to the circumlocution.Prefer the short word to the long.Prefer the Saxon word to the Romance.[1]" — H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler ([[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶6)

> "Whether is sufficient; see under Rule 13." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-misuse-wikisource-the-free-online-libr]] @ ¶10)

> "They are all dodges of jargon, circumlocutions for evad-ing this or that simple statement;" — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 130)

> "The Concise Oxford Dictionary begins its definition of this word: "instance of a thing's occurring; usual state of affairs." In these two senses, the word is usually unnecessary." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-misuse-wikisource-the-free-online-libr]] @ ¶14)
