# Prefer concrete words

Prefer concrete expression when an abstraction hides the actual subject. ([[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶6; [[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 133; [[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-misuse-wikisource-the-free-online-libr]] @ ¶2)

## Key points

- The Fowlers prefer the concrete, Quiller-Couch recommends concrete nouns, and Strunk prefers definite statement over vague generality. ([[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶6; [[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 133; [[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-misuse-wikisource-the-free-online-libr]] @ ¶2)
- Quiller-Couch’s image example makes an abstraction tangible; he does not demonstrate that all abstract vocabulary must disappear. ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 138)
- The Fowlers warn that some abstract sentences need reconstruction, and that important shades of meaning must survive revision. ([[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶10; [[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶47)
