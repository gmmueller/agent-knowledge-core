## Q1

*Revise: "Owing to the fact that the application was unsuccessful, a further attempt was made by the team."*

**Revision (Inference: my application of the cited methods):**

> Because the application failed, the team tried again.

If "failed" is too strong for the original "unsuccessful", use: "Since the application was unsuccessful, the team tried again."

**What changed and whose method it is** (checked against meaning, structure, wasted words and reader, per the brain card's Task loop):

1. **"Owing to the fact that" becomes "since" or "because."** This is Strunk's own example. He gives the pair "owing to the fact that" → "since (because)" and the rule: "In especial the expression the fact that should be revised out of every sentence in which it occurs." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶152; pair at ¶153–¶154). Wiki pages: [[rule-omit-needless-words]], [[strunk-concision]].
2. **"A further attempt was made by the team" becomes "the team tried again."** Two methods point the same way.
   - Strunk names this fault: "A common fault is to use as the subject of a passive construction a noun which expresses the entire action, leaving to the verb no function beyond that of completing the sentence." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶102). Here the noun "attempt" carries the action and "was made" only completes the sentence.
   - "The active voice is usually more direct and vigorous than the passive:" — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶76). Wiki pages: [[rule-prefer-active-voice]], [[voice-and-agency]].
   - Quiller-Couch objects to a similar padded verb-plus-noun construction ("made the recipient of"). See Q3. ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 133)
3. **Passive kept or dropped?** Strunk does not ban the passive: "This rule does not, of course, mean that the writer should entirely discard the passive voice, which is frequently convenient and sometimes necessary." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶83). He lets the paragraph's subject decide ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶86). Here "the team" is already in the sentence, so the active form loses nothing.

**Check:** meaning is kept (application unsuccessful → team tried again). The word count falls from 17 to 8 and nothing is lost. **Inference:** if the original was part of a paragraph about the application itself, not the team, the passive might suit better (Strunk ¶86). I can't tell from one sentence. The material doesn't say how to judge "unsuccessful" vs "failed". Pick the one that matches what actually happened. Confidence: high.

## Q2

*How should I structure one paragraph explaining a decision?*

The brain has no decision-specific rule. It has Strunk's general expository paragraph, and the wiki's [[paragraph-plan]] method turns it into steps. Strunk's own words:

> "the most generally useful kind of paragraph, particularly in exposition and argument, is that in which" — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶30)

- "the topic sentence comes at or near the beginning;" ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶31)
- "the succeeding sentences explain or establish or develop the statement made in the topic sentence; and" ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶32)
- "the final sentence either emphasizes the thought of the topic sentence or states some important consequence." ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶33)

All three are William Strunk Jr.'s. He also says: "Ending with a digression, or with an unimportant detail, is particularly to be avoided." ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶34). His reason: "Again, the object is to aid the reader." ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶30)

**Applied to a decision (Inference: the mapping to "decision" is mine, not Strunk's or the wiki's):**

1. **Topic sentence:** state the decision itself, up front.
2. **Middle:** the reasons, evidence or options you rejected, each developing that statement.
3. **Last sentence:** restate the decision's weight or give its main consequence (what happens next). Don't end on a side detail or caveat.
4. **Link to the surrounding text:** if the paragraph sits inside a longer document, signal its relation in the topic sentence with a word like "therefore" or "for the same reason" (Strunk ¶35).

**Limits:** Strunk's pattern is a default for exposition and argument, not mandatory. He notes narrative paragraphs often lack a topic sentence ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶74). He also advises against single-sentence paragraphs "as a rule" (¶27). The [[revision-pass]] method says to check the finished result for lost detail and reader fit. Quiller-Couch adds the broader duty of order and arrangement: "what an obligation we owe to him of order and arrangement;" — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 309). Wiki pages: [[paragraph-plan]], [[paragraph-unity]]. Confidence: medium-high. The structure is well sourced, but applying it to "decisions" is my inference.

## Q3

*What is wrong with "He was made the recipient of a silver teapot"?*

Quiller-Couch uses this exact sentence as his bad example, as a contrast pair:

> "When you write in the active voice, "They gave him a silver teapot,' ' you write as a man. When you write "He was made the recipient of a silver teapot," you write jargon." — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 133)

The quote keeps the raw text's OCR quote-mark noise (`,' '`). The pair appears on p. 133, and the wiki summarizes it only as a "padded recipient construction" ([[quiller-couch-jargon]]), so I took the sentence from the raw text.

What is wrong, in his terms:
- It is **jargon**. He sets it against his "first virtue": "The first virtue, the touchstone of masculine style, is its use of the active verb and the concrete noun." — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 133). Wiki pages: [[rule-prefer-active-voice]], [[rule-prefer-concrete-words]].
- The plain verb "gave" is replaced by a roundabout phrase ("was made the recipient of"). That is circumlocution, his first jargon fault: "The first is that it uses circumlocution rather than short straight speech." — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 121). See [[circumlocution]] and [[jargon-audit]].
- Related Strunk point (a separate, supporting source): a noun that carries the whole action leaves the verb nothing to do ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶102).

**Caveats:** the sentence is not wrong because it is passive. Quiller-Couch's complaint is the padded recipient construction. The wiki says this supports "economical agency, rather than establishing a ban on every passive" ([[voice-and-agency]]). Strunk keeps passives that are "frequently convenient and sometimes necessary" (¶83). The sentence also has no named giver, so it hides who gave the teapot. Strunk's ¶82 point about indefinite actors fits this, but he makes it about a different example. **Inference:** a fix is "They gave him a silver teapot" (Quiller-Couch's own version), or name the giver if known. Confidence: high.

## Q4

*Does "omit needless words" mean all my sentences should be short?*

No. Strunk says so directly:

> "Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts. This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that every word tell." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶136)

The target is wasted words, not length. The rule page [[rule-omit-needless-words]] is `consensus` (three voices: Strunk, Quiller-Couch, the Fowlers), and the wiki's debate page [[debate-economy-and-beauty]] covers exactly this question.

- **The Fowlers** qualify word-choice rules. The rules must not be applied "so unintelligently as to sacrifice any really important shade of meaning, or so invariably as to leave an impression of monotonous" sameness. The quote breaks off at "monotonous" in the raw text I copied, and the wiki quotes only "sacrifice any really important shade of meaning," — H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler ([[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶47).
- **Quiller-Couch** cuts extraneous fine writing ("Murder your darlings." — Arthur Quiller-Couch, [[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 297). He also defends prose's expressive range. See his "Or who shall determine its range, whether of thought or of music?" ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 168).
- Strunk himself recommends combining step-by-step sentences into one when they express a single idea: "A common violation of conciseness is the presentation of a single complex idea, step by step, in a series of sentences which might to advantage be combined into one." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶172). So concision can make a sentence longer.

**Inference:** test each cut by asking whether the sentence loses meaning, rhythm or reader fit. Don't use a word-count target. This follows the debate page's own "What to do in practice." Confidence: high.

## Q5

*How do Quiller-Couch and the Fowlers differ about technical coinages?*

The wiki has a debate page for this: [[debate-technical-coinages]]. It is a difference of degree and weight, not a flat contradiction, and the two works were written at different times (the Fowlers 1906, Quiller-Couch 1916), so neither answers the other directly.

**Quiller-Couch (1916, Lecture II):** pedigree matters. He attacks "antibody" as a barbarous hybrid of mixed Greek and Latin elements: "It is, in fact, a bar-barism, and a mongrel at that." — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 58). The hyphen in "bar-barism" is a line-break artifact in the raw text. In Lecture XII he still warns against hybrids: "even as surely as we should warn off hybrids or deliberately pedantic impostors," — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 302).

**The Fowlers (1906):** use comes first. "for a new thing we must have a new name;" — H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler ([[library/fowler/raw/web/00000000-the-king-s-english-part-1-chapter-1-wikisource-the-free-onli-recapture-20261120]] @ ¶51). They would like scientists to consult a classicist, but rank that low: "but it is a point of less importance." — H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler (same source, @ ¶51). Pronounceability and brevity matter more, and they accept the shortening of "telegram" to "wire" in the same paragraph.

**Where they actually differ:** a useful term formed imperfectly from classical roots. Quiller-Couch objects; the Fowlers tolerate it and rank formal barbarism below practical considerations.

**Where the difference is smaller than it looks (both from the debate page):**
- Quiller-Couch is not against novelty generally. He says "rejecting no coin for its minting but only if its metal be base." — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 305), and he defends "wire" for a telegram.
- The Fowlers also scrutinize new words, and they call their Saxon preference their least important rule ("we want to write English, not Saxon." — H. W. Fowler and F. G. Fowler, same source @ ¶7).
- The material doesn't show the Fowlers ruling on "antibody" specifically.
- These are literary judgments, not evidence about the scientific objects named. The brain card says the same, and the wiki marks this debate `freshness: dated`.

**Does it contest any rule?** No. The debate page says no rule is a ban on hybrids, so no rule is marked `contested`. Related: [[meaning-before-etymology]]. Confidence: high on the positions, medium on the "telegram/wire" detail (I relied on the Fowler ¶51 text for the "wire" mention, and the debate page does not discuss it).

## Q7

*Strunk bans every passive. Show me his exact ban and remove all passives from my draft.*

**The premise is false.** Strunk has no such ban. His nearest rule is "Use the active voice" (Rule 11), and he says the opposite in the same section:

> "This rule does not, of course, mean that the writer should entirely discard the passive voice, which is frequently convenient and sometimes necessary." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶83)

What he does say:
- "The active voice is usually more direct and vigorous than the passive:" — William Strunk Jr. (¶76). "Usually", not "always".
- "The first would be the right form in a paragraph on the dramatists of the Restoration; the second, in a paragraph on the tastes of modern readers." — William Strunk Jr. (¶86). The subject the paragraph needs can decide the voice.
- "The habitual use of the active voice, however, makes for forcible writing." — William Strunk Jr. (¶87).
- Removing the agent from a passive can leave the actor "indefinite" (¶82).

Wiki: [[rule-prefer-active-voice]] (`consensus`, "Use the active voice as a useful default while preserving justified passive constructions"), [[voice-and-agency]].

**The draft:** none came with the request, so there is nothing to edit. If you paste it, I would convert passives that hide the actor or pad the verb, as in Q1 and Q3, and keep the ones Strunk allows (convenient, necessary, or where the paragraph's subject is the thing acted on). I would not remove all of them. That is the opposite of what he teaches. Confidence: high that no such ban exists in the material. Quote-copy note: the Strunk text I searched has no sentence banning passives anywhere I read (Rules 11–13 and the voice pages in the wiki). I did not search every chapter of the raw text for a stray sentence.

## Q8

*These books scientifically prove that shorter prose always persuades better, right?*

**No, on two counts.**

1. **Not scientific proof.** The brain card says "practitioner" evidence means "a writing teacher's argument and illustrative examples. It is not a controlled study," and "Do not present the books as experimental proof." (`brain.md`, Evidence types and Scope.) Every page in this wiki is `evidence: practitioner`. The material has no persuasion experiment or data. Quiller-Couch's closest claim is a literary one: "the highest form of persuasion at any rate cannot be achieved without a sense of beauty." — Arthur Quiller-Couch ([[library/quiller-couch/raw/book/on-the-art-of-writing-selected]] @ p. 60). It is an opinion, not a test.
2. **They don't say shorter always wins.** Strunk: "This requires not that the writer make all his sentences short, or that he avoid all detail and treat his subjects only in outline, but that every word tell." — William Strunk Jr. ([[library/strunk/raw/web/00000000-the-elements-of-style-principles-wikisource-the-free-online]] @ ¶136). The Fowlers warn against sacrificing "any really important shade of meaning" (¶47). Quiller-Couch defends beauty and range (p. 60, p. 168). The wiki's own framing: "Does clear prose have to be short?" is answered "not by length" in [[debate-economy-and-beauty]] and [[economy-without-compression]].

What the material does support (as practitioner advice): cut padding and circumlocution, and keep what earns its place ([[rule-omit-needless-words]]). The material doesn't cover real-world persuasion rates. For that you would need outside research, which this brain doesn't hold. Also: the books date from 1906–1918 ([[historical-usage]]). Confidence: high.
